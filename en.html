1What trading actually is

🧒Like I'm 10

Imagine you collect football stickers. On Monday, a rare sticker is worth 2 normal stickers. You trade for it. On Friday, everybody wants that rare sticker and now it is worth 5 normal stickers. You trade it away and you now have 5 stickers instead of 2. You didn't make a sticker — you just bought when it was cheap and sold when it was expensive. That is trading.

Trading means buying and selling things of value (called assets) to make a profit from their price changes.

A price is simply what people agree to pay for something right now. Prices move all the time because the number of people who want to buy and the number who want to sell keep changing.

Why do prices move? Supply and demand

  • Demand = how many people want to buy.
  • Supply = how many people want to sell.
flowchart LR
    A["More buyers than sellers<br/>(high demand)"] --> B["Buyers compete<br/>and offer more"] --> C["📈 Price goes UP"]
    D["More sellers than buyers<br/>(high supply)"] --> E["Sellers compete<br/>and accept less"] --> F["📉 Price goes DOWN"]

What makes people want to buy or sell? News (a central bank changes interest rates), company or project results, fear, excitement, big players moving money, and other traders reacting to all of that.

Where does a trader's profit come from?

A trader makes money only from the difference between the price they enter at and the price they exit at, minus costs (fees).

Profit = (Exit price − Entry price) × Quantity − Costs      ← when you BUY first

Nobody pays you just for "taking part". For short-term trading, one trader's gain is often another trader's loss, and the platforms collect fees from both sides. That is one reason it is hard to win consistently.

Trading vs investing

🏃 Trading 🌳 Investing
Time Minutes to weeks Months to decades
Goal Profit from price moves Grow wealth as the asset grows (and earn dividends/interest)
Ownership Often you don't own the asset (you trade a contract about its price) Usually you own the asset
Tools Charts, orders, leverage Research, patience, diversification
Activity High (many decisions) Low (few decisions)
ℹ️Note

Speculation is the word for "betting on which direction a price will move". Most trading is speculation. It is not the same as gambling in a casino, but without an edge (a real, tested advantage) and risk control, it can behave a lot like gambling.

Who is in the market?

  • Institutions — banks, hedge funds, pension funds, market makers. They trade huge amounts and do most of the volume.
  • Retail traders — individuals like you, trading from an app or a platform.
  • Market makers — companies that always offer to buy and sell, earning the small difference between the two prices.
  • Bots/algorithms — computer programs that trade automatically. A big share of all orders today come from algorithms.

2Markets, assets, pairs and prices

🧒Like I'm 10

A market is like a giant playground swap-meet. An asset is the thing being swapped (stickers, marbles). A pair is saying "how many marbles for one sticker?" — you always compare one thing against another.

Key words

  • Market — a place (today, almost always digital) where buyers and sellers meet. Examples: the forex market, the crypto market, the stock market.
  • Asset — anything with a price that can be traded: currencies, cryptocurrencies, company shares, gold, oil…
  • Instrument / symbol — the specific thing you trade on a platform, e.g. EURUSD or BTCUSDT.
  • Ticker — the short code of an asset: BTC (Bitcoin), ETH (Ether), EUR (euro), USD (US dollar), XAU (gold).

Pairs: base and quote

In forex and crypto you almost always trade a pair: one asset priced in another.

      EUR / USD = 1.0850
      ───   ───
       │     └── QUOTE currency: what you pay with
       └──────── BASE currency: what you are buying/selling

 "1 euro costs 1.0850 US dollars"

      BTC / USDT = 60,000
       │      └── QUOTE: USDT (a "stablecoin" meant to be worth 1 US dollar)
       └───────── BASE:  Bitcoin

 "1 bitcoin costs 60,000 USDT"
  • If you buy EURUSD you are buying euros and paying with dollars.
  • If you sell EURUSD you are selling euros and receiving dollars.

A stablecoin is a crypto token designed to keep a fixed value, usually 1 USD (examples: USDT, USDC). Crypto exchanges use them as "digital dollars".

Two prices, not one: bid and ask

When you look closely, there are always two prices:

Price Meaning Who uses it
Bid The highest price someone is willing to pay right now You sell at the bid
Ask (or offer) The lowest price someone is willing to sell at right now You buy at the ask

The ask is always a bit higher than the bid. The gap is the spread (Part 9).

   Bid 1.08500  |  Ask 1.08510      →  Spread = 0.00010 = 1 pip
   (you sell)       (you buy)
🧒Like I'm 10

A shop buys your old games for $8 and sells the same games for $10. The shop's "bid" is $8, its "ask" is $10. If you buy and instantly sell back, you lose $2. That $2 is the spread.

Small units: pips, points and ticks

  • Pip ("percentage in point") — the standard small move in forex. For most pairs it is the 4th decimal (0.0001). For pairs with the Japanese yen, it is the 2nd decimal (0.01).
    • EURUSD from 1.0850 → 1.0851 = +1 pip.
    • USDJPY from 150.20 → 150.21 = +1 pip.
  • Pipette / point — one-tenth of a pip (the 5th decimal). MT5 often shows prices with 5 decimals and calls the smallest step a point. So 10 points = 1 pip.
  • Tick / tick size — the smallest price step an instrument can move. In crypto, each pair has its own tick size (e.g. 0.01 USDT for BTCUSDT).

Quantity: lots (forex) and units (crypto)

  • Forex is traded in lots:
Lot name Size Units of base currency Value of 1 pip on EURUSD
Standard lot 1.00 100,000 ≈ $10
Mini lot 0.10 10,000 ≈ $1
Micro lot 0.01 1,000 ≈ $0.10
  • Crypto is traded in units of the coin, often small fractions: 0.00125 BTC is a normal order. Each exchange has a minimum order size and a step size (how many decimals are allowed).

  • Exotic pairs involve smaller economies: bigger spreads and sudden jumps.
  • Altcoins = any crypto that isn't Bitcoin. Many are small, very volatile (they jump around a lot) and some go to zero.

3Forex vs Crypto

🧒Like I'm 10

Forex is trading the money of countries — like swapping euros for dollars before a holiday, but doing it to profit from tiny changes. Crypto is trading digital coins that live on the internet — younger, wilder, open all night and all weekend.

Forex (FX, "foreign exchange") is the market for exchanging national currencies. It is the biggest financial market in the world — trillions of dollars change hands every day. Crypto (cryptocurrency) is digital money recorded on a blockchain (a shared public record that many computers keep in sync).

Topic 💱 Forex 🪙 Crypto

Opening hours 24 hours, 5 days a week (closed at weekends) 24/7/365
Typical daily move of a major ~0.3%–1% (EURUSD) ~2%–5% for BTC, often much more for altcoins
Who sets the price Big banks and liquidity providers; your broker shows you a price Buyers and sellers on each exchange's order book
Where you trade Through a broker (e.g. Exness) using a platform (e.g. MT5) On an exchange (e.g. Binance, MEXC)
Do you own the asset? Usually no — retail forex is mostly CFDs (contracts on the price) On spot you own the coins and can withdraw them; on futures you don't
Leverage Often high (1:30 in strictly regulated regions, up to 1:500+ offshore) Spot: none. Futures: up to 100x+ on some exchanges
What moves price Interest rates, inflation, economic data, politics Adoption, regulation news, big holders ("whales"), hype, overall risk mood
Regulation Mature, but varies by country and by broker entity Younger; rules differ a lot by country and change often
Special costs Spread, commission, swap (overnight fee) Trading fees (maker/taker), funding rate on perpetual futures, withdrawal/network fees

4Exchanges vs brokers (and spot vs derivatives)

🧒Like I'm 10

An exchange is a big open market square: sellers put up signs with their prices, buyers put up signs with theirs, and the square just matches them. A broker is a shopkeeper: you don't see the other people — you buy from and sell to the shop, and the shop deals with the big market behind the scenes.

Exchange

An exchange is a platform that matches buyers and sellers. Everybody's orders go into an order book — a live list of all waiting buy and sell orders — and a matching engine pairs them.

                ORDER BOOK  BTC/USDT
      ┌──────────────── SELL orders (asks) ────────────────┐
      │   60,030.00   0.80 BTC                              │
      │   60,020.00   1.20 BTC                              │
      │   60,010.00   0.45 BTC   ← best ask (lowest seller) │
      ├───────────────────── spread ────────────────────────┤
      │   60,000.00   0.60 BTC   ← best bid (highest buyer) │
      │   59,990.00   2.10 BTC                              │
      │   59,980.00   0.95 BTC                              │
      └──────────────── BUY orders (bids) ─────────────────┘

Binance and MEXC are crypto exchanges. They make money mostly from trading fees.

Broker

A broker is a company that gives you access to markets. In retail forex, the broker usually shows you a price and becomes the other side of your trade (or passes it to bigger partners called liquidity providers). Exness is a forex/CFD broker. Brokers make money from spreads, commissions and swaps — and some also profit when clients lose (see "B-book" in Part 19).

Spot vs derivatives

This is one of the most important ideas in the whole guide.

  • Spot — you really buy the asset and pay the full price. Buy 0.01 BTC on Binance Spot and you own 0.01 BTC. You can hold it or withdraw it to your own wallet. You can only lose what you paid.
  • Derivative — a contract whose value depends on (is "derived" from) the price of something else. You don't own the thing; you own a promise about its price.
Derivative What it is Where you'll meet it
CFD (Contract for Difference) You and the broker agree to exchange the difference in price between when you open and close. Exness / MT5: EURUSD, gold, indices, even BTC CFDs
Futures A contract to buy/sell at a set price on a future date. Regulated futures exchanges; crypto "delivery futures"
Perpetual futures ("perps") Crypto futures with no expiry date. A funding rate paid between longs and shorts keeps its price close to spot. Binance Futures, MEXC Futures
Options The right, but not the obligation, to buy/sell at a price. More advanced; skip for now

Derivatives make two things easy: short selling (profiting when the price falls — Part 7) and leverage (trading more than your money — Part 9). Both are powerful and dangerous.

⚠️Warning

With spot, the worst case is that your coin goes to zero. With leveraged derivatives, you can lose your entire deposit in minutes, sometimes from a single sudden move.

5Your accounts: Binance, MEXC, MT5 and Exness

🧒Like I'm 10

Binance and MEXC are two big crypto markets. Exness is a shop (broker) for forex and other things. MT5 is not a shop at all — it is the cash register / control panel app that lots of shops (including Exness) let you use.

🟡 Binance

  • The largest crypto exchange by trading volume.
  • Products: Spot, Margin, Futures (USDⓈ-M = settled in stablecoins, COIN-M = settled in the coin), plus savings, "Earn", etc.
  • Spot trading fee: base rate around 0.1% per trade (lower with BNB or VIP levels — check the current fee page).
  • Very well documented API (Part 21) and a testnet (a fake-money version for developers): Spot testnet at testnet.binance.vision.
  • Availability and allowed products depend on your country.

🔵 MEXC

  • A crypto exchange known for listing many new and small tokens early (more choice, but many of these tokens are very risky or illiquid).
  • Spot and Futures, typically low fees (promotional 0%-maker offers appear often — check current rates).
  • Its Spot API (v3) is very similar to Binance's, which makes it easy for code to support both. Futures API access for placing orders has had restrictions at times — always read the current documentation.

🖥️ MetaTrader 5 (MT5)

  • Software, made by MetaQuotes, that brokers give to their clients. It is not a broker and doesn't hold your money.
  • You log in with an account number, password and server given by your broker (for example, an Exness server).
  • Features: charts, 21 timeframes, many built-in indicators, one-click trading, Expert Advisors (EAs) = trading robots written in the MQL5 language, and a Strategy Tester for backtesting.
  • Available on Windows, Mac, web, Android and iOS. There is an official Python package (MetaTrader5) that talks to the desktop terminal (Windows).

🟢 Exness

  • A broker offering forex, metals (gold/silver), energies, indices, stocks and crypto as CFDs.
  • You trade through MT4, MT5 or Exness's own web terminal/app.
  • Several account types (e.g. Standard, Pro, Raw Spread, Zero) that differ in spread vs commission.
  • Offers very high leverage in some regions — which is a risk, not a gift.
  • Free demo accounts with virtual money: perfect for learning MT5.
  • Regulation depends on which Exness entity your account belongs to — check it in your account.

6Charts and candlesticks

🧒Like I'm 10

A price chart is like a diary of the price. Each candle is one page of the diary: it tells you where the price started, where it ended, and the highest and lowest places it visited during that time.

Chart types

  • Line chart — joins the closing prices with a line. Simple, hides detail.
  • Bar chart (OHLC) — each period is a vertical bar with small ticks for open and close.
  • Candlestick chart — the most popular. Same information as a bar, but easier to read.

Anatomy of a candle

Every candle shows 4 prices for one time period — OHLC:

  • Open — the first price of the period
  • High — the highest price reached
  • Low — the lowest price reached
  • Close — the last price of the period
  • Body — the thick part between open and close. A big body = strong move in one direction.
  • Wicks (shadows) — the thin lines. A long wick means price went there but was pushed back — a sign of rejection.
  • Green / bullish candle — close is higher than open. Bullish = rising (a bull attacks by pushing its horns up).
  • Red / bearish candle — close is lower than open. Bearish = falling (a bear swipes its paws down).

Timeframes

A timeframe is how much time each candle represents: 1 minute (M1), 5 minutes (M5), 15 minutes (M15), 1 hour (H1), 4 hours (H4), 1 day (D1), 1 week (W1)…

The same market looks completely different on different timeframes: a strong rise on M5 might be a tiny bump on D1. Beginners usually do better starting with higher timeframes (H4, D1): fewer, slower, less noisy decisions.

A few common candle patterns

Pattern What it looks like What people think it means
Doji Open ≈ close, tiny body, wicks both sides Indecision: buyers and sellers are balanced
Hammer Small body at the top, long lower wick Sellers pushed down, buyers pushed back up → possible bounce
Shooting star Small body at the bottom, long upper wick Buyers pushed up, sellers pushed back down → possible drop
Bullish engulfing A green body that completely covers the previous red body Buyers took over
Bearish engulfing A red body that completely covers the previous green body Sellers took over
⚠️Warning

Patterns are hints, not promises. On their own they are wrong very often. They mean much more when they appear at an important place on the chart (like support or resistance — Part 10).

Volume

Volume is how much was traded in a period, usually shown as bars under the chart. A price move with high volume has more "people behind it" than the same move on low volume.

7Long vs Short

🧒Like I'm 10
  • Going long = "I think this toy will get more popular." You buy it now, sell it later for more.
  • Going short = "I think this toy will get less popular." You borrow one from a friend, sell it now for $10, then when it drops to $6 you buy one back, return it to your friend, and keep the $4.
  • Long (buy) — you profit when the price rises.
  • Short (sell) — you profit when the price falls.
📈 Long 📉 Short
Opening action Buy Sell
Closing action Sell Buy back
You win if price… Goes up Goes down
Opens at… Ask price Bid price
Maximum possible loss (no leverage) What you paid (price can go to 0) Unlimited in theory (price can keep rising)
Formula (Exit − Entry) × Size (Entry − Exit) × Size

Where can you short?

  • Forex / CFDs on MT5 (Exness): yes, just press Sell. Every forex trade is really a long on one currency and a short on the other — selling EURUSD = short euro, long dollar.
  • Crypto spot (Binance/MEXC): you can only sell coins you already own. Selling your BTC is "exiting", not shorting.
  • Crypto futures/margin: yes, you can open a short.
ℹ️Note

In MT5, "Buy" opens a long and "Sell" opens a short. To close, you don't press the opposite button — you close the position (the platform then does the opposite trade for you).

8Order types

🧒Like I'm 10

An order is an instruction to the market.

  • Market order: "Give me one now, whatever it costs!"
  • Limit order: "I'll only buy if it's $5 or cheaper."
  • Stop order: "If it reaches $8, then buy/sell it for me." (a trigger)
  • Stop-loss: "If things go badly, get me out at this point."
  • Take-profit: "If things go well, cash out at this point."

An order is your instruction to buy or sell. A position is what you hold after an order is filled. A fill (or execution) means the order actually happened.

The main order types

Order What it does Pros Cons
Market Buy/sell immediately at the best price available Always fills (if there is liquidity) Price not guaranteed → slippage
Limit Buy at this price or lower / sell at this price or higher Price guaranteed or better Might never fill
Stop (stop-market) When price touches the stop price, send a market order Enter breakouts, exit losses automatically Can fill worse than the stop price in fast markets
Stop-limit When price touches the stop, place a limit order Controls worst price Might not fill at all if price jumps past
Stop-Loss (SL) A stop order that closes your position to limit the loss Protects your account Can be hit by a short spike ("stop hunt"), slippage in gaps
Take-Profit (TP) A limit order that closes your position at your target Locks in profit without watching Price may reverse just before it
Trailing stop A stop-loss that follows price at a set distance as it moves in your favour Lets winners run Can close on normal wiggles if too tight
OCO (One-Cancels-the-Other) Two orders linked: when one fills, the other is cancelled Place TP and SL together on spot exchanges Slightly more complex

  • Sell Limit — above current price (sell the rally).
  • Buy Stop — above current price (buy the breakout).
  • Sell Stop — below current price (sell the breakdown).

Extra words you'll see on exchanges

  • Maker — your order adds liquidity by waiting in the book (usually a limit order). Makers often pay lower fees.
  • Taker — your order takes liquidity by matching immediately (market orders, or limits that match at once). Takers pay higher fees.
  • Time in force — how long an order stays alive:
    • GTC (Good-Til-Canceled) — until filled or you cancel.
    • IOC (Immediate-Or-Cancel) — fill what you can now, cancel the rest.
    • FOK (Fill-Or-Kill) — fill everything now or nothing.
  • Post-only — "only accept my limit order if it will be a maker" (never a taker).
  • Reduce-only (futures) — the order can only make your position smaller, never open a new one. Very useful for bots.

9Spread, fees, leverage, margin, liquidity and volatility

These six words decide how much trading costs you and how fast you can lose. Learn them well.

9.1 Spread

🧒Like I'm 10

The spread is the "shop's cut" — the gap between the buying price and the selling price. You start every trade slightly losing because of it.

Spread = Ask − Bid.

  • EURUSD bid 1.08500 / ask 1.08510 → spread 1.0 pip.
  • On a 0.10 lot trade (≈$1 per pip) that costs about $1 to enter.
  • Spreads are small for popular pairs (EURUSD) and big for exotic pairs, small altcoins, and during news or at the daily rollover (around 21:00–22:00 UTC).

9.2 Fees and other costs

Cost Where How it works
Spread Everywhere, especially brokers Built into the price
Commission Brokers (raw/zero-spread accounts) Fixed amount per lot, e.g. a few dollars per lot per side
Trading fee (maker/taker) Crypto exchanges % of trade value, e.g. 0.1% of $500 = $0.50
Swap / rollover Forex/CFDs held past the daily rollover Interest difference between the two currencies; can be paid or received. Some accounts are "swap-free".
Funding rate Crypto perpetual futures Every few hours (often every 8h) longs pay shorts or shorts pay longs. E.g. 0.01% on $2,000 = $0.20 per funding.
Withdrawal / network fee Crypto withdrawals Fee to send coins on the blockchain
Slippage Everywhere Getting a worse price than expected (not a fee, but a real cost)
ℹ️Note

Costs look tiny per trade, but a bot that trades 20 times a day pays them 20 times a day. Many strategies that look profitable on paper become losers after costs.

9.3 Leverage

🧒Like I'm 10

Leverage is like a magnifying glass for money. Put $10 under a 10x glass and it looks like $100. If the price moves a little in your favour, your win is 10x bigger. If it moves a little against you, your loss is 10x bigger — and the glass can burn your $10 to nothing.

Leverage lets you control a position bigger than your own money. It is written as a ratio (1:100) or a multiplier (100x).

Position size (notional) = Your margin × Leverage
    $100 × 10  = $1,000 position
    $100 × 100 = $10,000 position

The notional value is the full size of the position in money.

Price move against you 1x (no leverage) 10x 50x 100x
−1% −1% of your money −10% −50% −100% 💥
−2% −2% −20% −100% 💥 💥
−5% −5% −50% 💥 💥
−10% −10% −100% 💥 💥 💥
🛑Caution

Leverage doesn't change the market. It changes how much each move matters to you. The key to safety is position size and a stop-loss — not the leverage number displayed in your account. (Example 1 in Part 18 shows how a trader with 1:100 leverage available can still risk only 1%.)

9.4 Margin

Margin is the money the platform locks as a deposit while your leveraged position is open.

Required margin = Notional value ÷ Leverage
   EURUSD 0.10 lot = 10,000 EUR ≈ $10,850 notional
   at 1:100 → margin ≈ $108.50

Words you'll see in MT5:

  • Balance — money in the account, not counting open trades.
  • Equity — balance +/− profit/loss of open trades. ("What you'd have if you closed everything now.")
  • Used margin — money locked by open positions.
  • Free margin — equity − used margin (what's left to open new trades or absorb losses).
  • Margin level — Equity ÷ Used margin × 100%.
  • Margin call — a warning when margin level falls to a threshold (varies by broker).
  • Stop out — the broker automatically closes your positions when margin level falls to a lower threshold, to stop your account going negative.

In crypto futures the same idea is called liquidation: if your losses eat your margin down to the maintenance margin, the exchange force-closes your position and you lose that margin.

Cross vs isolated margin (crypto futures):

  • Isolated — each position has its own margin. If it's liquidated, only that margin is lost.
  • Cross — all your futures balance backs all positions. One bad position can drain everything.

9.5 Liquidity

🧒Like I'm 10

Liquidity is how easy it is to swap something quickly at a fair price. Popular stickers — lots of kids want them — easy to swap. A weird sticker nobody wants — you might wait days or have to accept a bad deal.

  • High liquidity (EURUSD, BTC/USDT): tight spreads, big orders fill without moving price much.
  • Low liquidity (exotic pairs, tiny altcoins): wide spreads, slippage, and sometimes you can't exit when you want.

Slippage = the difference between the price you expected and the price you got. It happens with market and stop orders when the book is thin or price moves fast.

Order book depth = how much volume is waiting at each price. Deep book = high liquidity.

9.6 Volatility

Volatility is how much and how fast a price moves.

  • High volatility = big swings = bigger possible wins and losses, stops hit more often.
  • Low volatility = calm, small moves.
  • Volatility jumps around news (e.g. US jobs report, central bank decisions), market opens, and in crypto around big announcements or liquidations.
  • A common way to measure it is the ATR indicator (Part 11).

10Technical analysis fundamentals

🧒Like I'm 10

Technical analysis is like looking at footprints in the sand to guess where someone is walking. You can't be sure, but if the footprints keep going up the beach, they're probably still going up.

There are three ways traders try to understand prices:

Analysis Looks at Example question
Fundamental analysis The "real world": economy, interest rates, company or project news "Will the central bank raise rates?"
Sentiment analysis What the crowd feels "Is everyone extremely greedy right now?"
Technical analysis (TA) The chart: price, volume, patterns "Is price near a level where it bounced before?"

TA is the most popular for short-term trading and for bots, because price data is easy to get and easy for a computer to process.

The core beliefs of TA:

  1. Price already reflects much of the known information.
  2. Prices tend to move in trends.
  3. Human behaviour (fear, greed) repeats, so some patterns repeat.

None of these is guaranteed. TA gives probabilities, never certainties.

10.1 Trends and market structure

  • Uptrend — price makes Higher Highs (HH) and Higher Lows (HL). Like climbing stairs.
  • Downtrend — Lower Highs (LH) and Lower Lows (LL). Like walking down stairs.
  • Range / sideways — price bounces between a floor and a ceiling.

"The trend is your friend" — many strategies only trade in the direction of the bigger trend.

10.2 Support and resistance

  • Support — a price area where falling prices tend to stop and bounce, because buyers show up there. A floor.
  • Resistance — a price area where rising prices tend to stop and turn down, because sellers show up there. A ceiling.
  • They are zones, not exact lines.
  • When a resistance is clearly broken, it often becomes new support (role reversal), and vice versa.
  • Round numbers (1.1000 in EURUSD, 60,000 in BTC) often act as support/resistance because many people put orders there.

10.3 Breakouts and fakeouts

  • Breakout — price moves through support or resistance with strength (often with high volume).
  • Fakeout (false breakout) — price pokes through, then quickly comes back. Very common. Many traders wait for a candle close beyond the level, or a retest of the level, before trusting a breakout.

10.4 Trendlines and channels

A trendline connects 2+ swing lows in an uptrend (or swing highs in a downtrend). A channel adds a parallel line on the other side. They help you see the "slope" of a trend.

10.5 Multi-timeframe analysis

Look at the big picture first, then zoom in:

11Indicators and signals

🧒Like I'm 10

Indicators are like the gauges in a car dashboard: speed, fuel, temperature. They're calculated from the price, so they help you read the car — but they don't drive it, and they can't see the road ahead.

An indicator is a math formula applied to price (and/or volume) that produces a line or bars on the chart.

  • Lagging indicators confirm what already happened (moving averages). Reliable but late.
  • Leading indicators / oscillators try to show momentum changes early (RSI). Early but often wrong.

A signal is a specific rule that says "act now", e.g. "RSI crossed back above 30". A confirmation is a second, independent clue that agrees with the first.

The essential indicators

Indicator What it measures Common settings Typical way it's read
SMA — Simple Moving Average Average closing price of the last N candles 20, 50, 200 Price above the 200 SMA → long-term uptrend
EMA — Exponential Moving Average Like SMA but recent prices count more (reacts faster) 9, 21, 50 Fast EMA crossing above slow EMA → possible uptrend start
RSI — Relative Strength Index Speed of recent gains vs losses, scale 0–100 14 > 70 "overbought", < 30 "oversold"
MACD Difference between two EMAs + a signal line 12, 26, 9 MACD line crossing above signal line → bullish momentum
Bollinger Bands A moving average with bands 2 standard deviations away 20, 2 Bands squeeze = low volatility (often before a big move)
ATR — Average True Range Average size of a candle's range → volatility 14 Used to place stops: e.g. SL = 1.5 × ATR away
Volume How much is traded — Breakouts with rising volume are more convincing

How a moving average works (tiny example)

Last 5 closing prices: 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 → 5-period SMA = (10+11+12+13+14) ÷ 5 = 12. Next candle closes at 15 → drop the oldest (10), add 15 → (11+12+13+14+15) ÷ 5 = 13. The average "moves" with each new candle — that's why it's called a moving average.

Important truths about indicators

⚠️Warning
  • Indicators are built from price. They can't know anything price doesn't already know.
  • "Overbought" does not mean "about to fall". In a strong uptrend RSI can stay above 70 for weeks.
  • Adding more indicators usually doesn't add more information — many say the same thing. 2–3 well-understood tools beat 10 confusing ones.
  • Every setting (14, 20, 50…) is a choice. If you try hundreds of settings on old data, one will look amazing by pure luck (overfitting, Part 20).

12Basic trading strategies

🧒Like I'm 10

A strategy is a recipe. It says exactly: when to start cooking (entry), how much to cook (size), when it's done (take-profit), and when to throw it away (stop-loss). Without a recipe, you're just throwing things in a pan.

A trading strategy is a complete set of rules:

flowchart LR
    M["1️⃣ Market & timeframe<br/>what and when to watch"] --> S["2️⃣ Setup<br/>conditions to look for"]
    S --> E["3️⃣ Entry<br/>exact trigger"]
    E --> R["4️⃣ Risk<br/>stop-loss & position size"]
    R --> X["5️⃣ Exit<br/>take-profit / trailing / time"]
    X --> J["6️⃣ Review<br/>journal & statistics"]

If a rule is not written down precisely, it isn't a strategy yet. Precise rules are also exactly what a bot needs — this is the bridge to your future project.

Trading styles (by holding time)

Style Holding time Timeframes Notes
Scalping Seconds–minutes M1–M5 Costs matter enormously; very stressful; mostly for experts/bots with low fees
Day trading Minutes–hours, closed same day M5–H1 No overnight swap, needs lots of screen time
Swing trading Days–weeks H4–D1 Fewer decisions; often most suitable for beginners learning
Position trading Weeks–months D1–W1 Close to investing

Five basic strategy families

1. Trend following — "Ride the wave."

  • Idea: find a trend and trade in its direction.
  • Example rules: Only go long when price is above the 200 SMA. Enter when the 20 EMA crosses above the 50 EMA. Stop-loss below the last swing low. Exit when the 20 EMA crosses back below the 50 EMA.
  • Strength: catches big moves. Weakness: many small losses in sideways markets.

2. Range trading (support & resistance) — "Buy the floor, sell the ceiling."

  • Idea: in a sideways market, buy near support, sell near resistance.
  • Stop-loss just outside the range. Weakness: when the range finally breaks, it can break hard.

3. Breakout trading — "Jump on when it escapes."

  • Idea: when price breaks out of a range or pattern with strong volume, trade in the breakout direction.
  • Often uses Buy Stop / Sell Stop orders. Weakness: fakeouts.

4. Mean reversion — "What goes too far tends to come back."

  • Idea: when price stretches far from its average (e.g. outside a Bollinger Band, RSI < 30), bet on a return to the average.
  • Weakness: in a strong trend, "too far" can go much further.

5. Dollar-Cost Averaging (DCA) — technically investing, not trading.

  • Buy a fixed amount at regular times (e.g. $50 every week) regardless of price.
  • Removes timing decisions; popular for long-term crypto holders. Many exchanges offer "recurring buy" features and DCA bots.

Bonus — Grid trading (popular with bots): place many buy orders below and sell orders above the current price at fixed intervals, profiting from back-and-forth movement. Works in ranges, suffers badly in strong one-way trends.

ℹ️Note

No strategy works all the time. Trend strategies struggle in ranges; range strategies struggle in trends. What matters is whether, over hundreds of trades and after costs, the strategy has a positive expectancy (Part 13) — and whether you can follow it.

13Risk management

🧒Like I'm 10

Imagine a game where you start with 100 marbles. Rule #1: never put more than 1 or 2 marbles on any single round. Then even if you lose ten rounds in a row — which will happen sometimes — you still have more than 80 marbles and can keep playing and learning. The kid who bets 50 marbles per round is out of the game after two bad rounds.

Risk management is the most important part of trading. Good traders are wrong often; they survive because their losses are small and planned.

13.1 The 1% rule

Risk a small, fixed percentage of your account on each trade — commonly 0.5%–2%, and 1% is a good learning default.

"Risk" here means: how much you lose if your stop-loss is hit — not the size of the position.

Risk per trade Account after 10 losses in a row (start = 1,000)
1% ≈ 904 (−9.6%)
2% ≈ 817 (−18.3%)
5% ≈ 599 (−40.1%)
10% ≈ 349 (−65.1%)

Ten losses in a row sounds rare, but over hundreds of trades, long losing streaks are normal.

13.2 Position sizing — the most useful formula in this guide

                      Account × Risk %
Position size  =  ─────────────────────────────
                  Distance to stop-loss × Value per unit of distance

In plain words: decide how much money you're willing to lose first, then calculate how big the trade can be.

  • Crypto spot example: account 1,000 USDT, risk 1% = 10 USDT. Entry 60,000, stop 58,800 → distance 1,200 USDT per BTC. Size = 10 ÷ 1,200 = 0.00833 BTC.
  • Forex example: account $1,000, risk 1% = $10. Stop 30 pips away. Needed pip value = 10 ÷ 30 = $0.333/pip → 0.0333 lots → round down to 0.03 lots (because 0.01 lot = $0.10/pip on EURUSD).

13.3 Risk-to-reward ratio (R:R)

Compare what you risk (entry → stop) with what you aim to gain (entry → target).

  • Risk 30 pips, target 60 pips → 1:2.
  • Traders often call the risk amount 1R. A win of 60 pips = +2R; a loss = −1R.

A good R:R means you don't need to win often:

R:R Win rate needed just to break even (before costs)
1:1 50%
1:1.5 40%
1:2 33.3%
1:3 25% 
Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + reward/risk)

13.4 Expectancy — does the strategy make money on average?

Expectancy (in R) = (Win rate × Average win in R) − (Loss rate × Average loss in R)

Example: win rate 40%, average win +2R, average loss −1R: 0.40 × 2 − 0.60 × 1 = 0.80 − 0.60 = +0.20R per trade. Risking $10 per trade (1R = $10) → about +$2 per trade on average, over many trades, before costs.

A strategy with a negative expectancy will lose money in the long run no matter how clever it feels.

13.5 Drawdown and why losses hurt more than gains help

Drawdown = how far your account falls from its highest point.

Loss Gain needed to get back to start
−10% +11.1%
−20% +25%
−30% +42.9%
−50% +100%
−75% +300%
−90% +900%

13.6 A beginner's risk checklist

  • Every trade has a stop-loss decided before entering.
  • Position size calculated from the 1% rule.
  • R:R of at least 1:1.5–1:2 (for most setups).
  • A daily loss limit (e.g. stop trading for the day after −3%).
  • Don't open several trades that are really the same bet (e.g. long EURUSD + long GBPUSD + short USDCHF are all "short the dollar" — this is correlation).
  • Know the news calendar (big events can jump over stops).
  • Never add money to a losing position just to "average down" without a plan.
  • Only trade money you can truly afford to lose.

15Trading psychology

🧒Like I'm 10

Your brain is great at running away from lions, but bad at trading. When you're losing, it screams "don't accept it, maybe it comes back!" When you're winning, it screams "take it now before it disappears!" That's backwards from what good trading needs.

The main emotional traps

Trap What it feels like What it causes
Fear "What if it drops more?" Closing winners too early, not taking valid trades
Greed "It could go 10x!" Oversized positions, no take-profit, too much leverage
FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out) "Everyone's making money, I need to get in NOW!" Buying at the top after a huge rise
Hope "It'll come back…" Moving or removing stop-losses, holding losers forever
Revenge trading "I'll win it back right now!" Big, impulsive trades after a loss → bigger losses
Overconfidence "I've won 5 in a row, I'm a genius." Increasing size right before a losing streak
Loss aversion Losses hurt ~2x more than equal gains feel good Cutting winners, holding losers

Habits that help

  1. Write a trading plan before the market opens: what you'll trade, the rules, max risk.
  2. Keep a trading journal: every trade, why you took it, screenshot, result, how you felt. (Great first software project, see Part 24!)
  3. Think in series, not single trades. One trade means nothing; 100 trades show your edge.
  4. Set limits: max trades per day, max daily loss, and walk away when reached.
  5. Accept that losses are a cost of doing business, like a shop paying rent.
  6. Automate the rules you break — this is one honest reason people build bots: a bot doesn't panic. (But a bot also doesn't think, so it must be tested and watched.)

16Common beginner mistakes and risks

Trading mistakes

  1. ❌ No stop-loss — "I'll watch it." Then you fall asleep, or freeze.
  2. ❌ Risking too much per trade — one bad day ends the account.
  3. ❌ Using maximum leverage because the broker allows it.
  4. ❌ Overtrading — too many trades, often out of boredom; costs pile up.
  5. ❌ Moving the stop-loss further away when price approaches it.
  6. ❌ Averaging down on losers without a plan.
  7. ❌ Chasing after a huge candle (FOMO).
  8. ❌ Trading during major news without understanding spreads widen and prices gap.
  9. ❌ Strategy-hopping — changing method after every loss, so you never learn if anything works.
  10. ❌ Ignoring costs — spread, fees, swaps, funding.
  11. ❌ Trusting "signals" groups, influencers or "guaranteed profit" bots.
  12. ❌ Skipping the demo phase.

Scams and platform risks (especially in crypto)

🛑Caution
  • "Guaranteed returns" or "risk-free 5% per day" → always a scam.
  • Fake exchanges / fake apps and phishing links that steal your password or API keys. Always type the URL yourself and turn on 2FA (two-factor authentication).
  • Pump-and-dump coins: a group pushes a tiny coin up, then sells to latecomers.
  • "Account managers" who message you on Telegram/WhatsApp asking for access or deposits.
  • Exchange risk: an exchange can freeze withdrawals, be hacked or collapse (FTX, 2022). Coins kept on an exchange are custodial — the exchange controls them.
  • Unregulated brokers may refuse withdrawals.
  • Leaked API keys can be used to trade your account into losses. Never give a key withdrawal permission (Part 21).

18Complete practical examples

📌Important

These examples are for learning the mechanics. Prices are illustrative and simplified. They are not trade recommendations. Real fees, spreads and minimum sizes depend on your account type and change over time.

Example 1 — Forex: a long EURUSD trade on Exness MT5 (demo account)

Situation. You have an Exness demo account (Standard type) with $1,000 and leverage 1:100. On the H4 chart, EURUSD has been in an uptrend (higher highs, higher lows) and has pulled back to an old resistance area around 1.0830, which now looks like support. A bullish engulfing candle just closed there.

Step 1 — Read the prices. Bid 1.08500 / Ask 1.08510 → spread = 0.00010 = 1 pip. A long opens at the ask (1.08510) and will later close at the bid.

Step 2 — Choose the stop-loss (where the idea is wrong). Below the support zone and the engulfing candle's low: 1.08210. Distance = 1.08510 − 1.08210 = 0.00300 = 30 pips.

Step 3 — Choose the take-profit. Near the recent high: 1.09110. Distance = 0.00600 = 60 pips. R:R = 30 : 60 = 1:2.

Step 4 — Decide the risk. 1% of $1,000 = $10 maximum loss.

Step 5 — Calculate the position size.

  • Needed pip value = $10 ÷ 30 pips = $0.333 per pip.
  • On EURUSD, 0.01 lot ≈ $0.10 per pip → 0.0333 lots.
  • MT5 volume step is 0.01 → round down to 0.03 lots (rounding up would risk more than 1%).
  • Real pip value = 0.03 × $10 = $0.30 per pip.
  • Real risk = 30 × $0.30 = $9.00 (0.9%) ✅.

Step 6 — Check margin (leverage).

  • 0.03 lots = 3,000 EUR. Notional ≈ 3,000 × 1.0851 = $3,255.30.
  • Margin at 1:100 = 3,255.30 ÷ 100 = $32.55 locked.
  • Free margin ≈ $1,000 − $32.55 = $967.45. Margin level ≈ 1,000 ÷ 32.55 ≈ 3,072% — very safe.
  • Notice: 1:100 leverage was available, but the trade uses only ~3.3x effective leverage ($3,255 / $1,000). Position size, not the leverage setting, decided the risk.

Step 7 — Place the order in MT5. New Order → Symbol EURUSD → Type Market Execution → Volume 0.03 → Stop Loss 1.08210 → Take Profit 1.09110 → Buy by Market. (Or use a Buy Limit at a lower price to wait for a better entry.)

Step 8 — Execution. Filled at 1.08510 (on a calm market; in fast markets you could get slippage).

Step 9 — Monitoring. The position shows floating P/L. At first it shows about −$0.30 — that's the 1-pip spread you paid. You don't move the stop further away. If the trade is still open at the daily rollover, a swap (small plus or minus) is applied.

Step 10 — Closing: three possible endings.

Outcome Close price (bid) Pips Result
🎯 Take-profit hit 1.09110 +60 +$18.00 (+1.8%)
🛑 Stop-loss hit 1.08210 −30 −$9.00 (−0.9%), maybe a bit more with slippage
✋ Manual close 1.08810 +30 +$9.00

Plus/minus any swap. On a Standard account the spread was the main cost; on Raw/Zero accounts you'd see a smaller spread but a commission per lot.

Step 11 — Journal. Write: setup, screenshot, planned vs actual, emotions, result in R (+2R or −1R).

Example 2 — Crypto: a spot BTC/USDT trade on Binance with an OCO exit

Situation. You hold 1,000 USDT on Binance Spot (no leverage, no shorting). On the daily chart, BTC is in an uptrend and is pulling back toward a support zone around 60,000 that held twice before.

Step 1 — Plan.

  • Entry: Buy Limit at 60,000 (wait for price to come to you → you're a maker).
  • Stop-loss: 58,800 (−2%, under the support zone).
  • Take-profit: 62,400 (+4%).
  • R:R = 1,200 : 2,400 = 1:2.

Step 2 — Risk. 1% of 1,000 = 10 USDT.

Step 3 — Position size.

  • Risk per 1 BTC = 60,000 − 58,800 = 1,200 USDT.
  • Size = 10 ÷ 1,200 = 0.0083333 BTC → exchange step size 0.00001 → 0.00833 BTC.
  • Cost = 0.00833 × 60,000 = 499.80 USDT (the other ~500 USDT stays in your account).

Step 4 — Place the limit order. Spot → BTC/USDT → Limit → Buy → Price 60,000 → Amount 0.00833 → Buy BTC. The order now waits in the order book.

Step 5 — Execution. Two days later, price dips to 59,950 and your order fills at 60,000 (a limit order never fills at a worse price). Fee: 0.1% × 499.80 = ≈0.50 USDT.

ℹ️Note

On Binance, the buy fee is taken from what you receive (a little BTC) unless you pay fees with BNB. To keep the maths simple here we count all fees in USDT.

Step 6 — Attach the exits with an OCO order. Spot orders can't carry SL/TP like MT5, so you place an OCO sell for 0.00833 BTC:

  • Limit sell (take-profit) at 62,400.
  • Stop-limit sell: stop 58,800, limit 58,700 (a bit lower, to improve the chance it fills in a fast drop). When one fills, the exchange cancels the other.

Step 7 — Possible endings.

Outcome Sell value Gross P/L Fees (buy + sell) Net P/L
🎯 TP at 62,400 0.00833 × 62,400 = 519.79 +19.99 0.50 + 0.52 = 1.02 ≈ +18.97 USDT (+1.9%)
🛑 SL at ≈58,800 0.00833 × 58,800 = 489.80 −10.00 0.50 + 0.49 = 0.99 ≈ −10.99 USDT (−1.1%)

Look at the fees: about 1 USDT per round trip, which is ~10% of the planned risk. For a bot trading many times per day, this matters a lot.

Step 8 — What could go wrong?

  • The limit buy never fills (price never dips) → no trade, no loss. That's fine.
  • A crash gaps through 58,700 → the stop-limit might not fill, and you still hold BTC while it falls. (A stop-market would fill, but at a worse price.)
  • Since it's spot, you can't be liquidated and can't lose more than the 499.80 USDT spent.

Example 3 — Why leverage is dangerous: a BTC perpetual futures short

Situation. You put 100 USDT into Binance USDⓈ-M Futures, isolated margin, and choose 20x leverage. You think BTC will fall and short at 60,000.

Step 1 — Size. Notional = 100 × 20 = 2,000 USDT → 2,000 ÷ 60,000 = 0.0333 BTC short.

Step 2 — How each move feels.

BTC price Move P/L on 0.0333 BTC short Effect on your 100 USDT
57,000 −5% +100 USDT +100% 🤩
59,400 −1% +20 USDT +20%
60,600 +1% −20 USDT −20%
~62,700 ≈ +4.5% ≈ −90 USDT Liquidated 💥 (the rest goes to fees / insurance fund)

The liquidation happens before the full 5% (100 ÷ 2,000) because the exchange needs to keep a maintenance margin. The exact liquidation price is shown on the exchange's order screen.

Step 3 — Funding. If the funding rate is +0.01% (longs pay shorts), every 8 hours you receive 0.01% × 2,000 = 0.20 USDT. If it's negative, you pay. On volatile days funding can be much larger.

Lesson. A normal ~4–5% daily move in BTC is enough to wipe out a 20x position.

The same idea, sized with the 1% rule: say your whole futures balance is 1,000 USDT. You risk 1% = 10 USDT and put the stop-loss at 60,600 (600 USDT above entry, i.e. 1%).

  • Size = 10 ÷ 600 = 0.0167 BTC → notional ≈ 1,000 USDT → only ~1x effective leverage.
  • If the stop is hit, you lose ≈ 10 USDT + fees — not the whole account.
  • The leverage setting only decides how much margin is locked; the stop-loss and position size decide the risk.
🛑Caution

The exchange lets you choose up to 100x+ leverage. The platform allowing it doesn't make it sensible. Liquidation cascades (many traders being force-closed at once) cause the sudden huge candles you'll often see in crypto.

19.3 What "the price" really is

20Trading bots and automation

🧒Like I'm 10

A trading bot is a robot that follows your recipe exactly — it never gets tired, scared or greedy. But it's also not smart: if the recipe is bad, it will cook bad food very fast and very consistently. And if the kitchen catches fire, it keeps cooking unless you taught it to stop.

A trading bot is a program that reads market data, decides using rules, and sends orders automatically. An algorithm is the set of rules. Algorithmic trading (algo trading) is trading with such programs.

20.1 The parts of a bot

flowchart LR
    D["📡 Market data<br/>candles, ticks, order book<br/>(REST + WebSocket)"] --> S["🧠 Strategy<br/>indicators → signals"]
    S --> R["🛡️ Risk manager<br/>position size, max loss,<br/>kill switch"]
    R --> E["📤 Execution<br/>send / modify / cancel orders"]
    E --> V["🏛️ Exchange / Broker"]
    V -- "fills, balances,<br/>order updates" --> P["📒 Portfolio & state<br/>positions, P/L"]
    P --> R
    P --> L["📊 Logging, alerts,<br/>dashboard"]
  1. Data feed — gets prices (historical and live).
  2. Strategy — turns data into signals ("go long", "exit").
  3. Risk manager — the most important piece: can veto any trade (too big, daily loss reached, too many open positions, abnormal price).
  4. Execution engine — turns decisions into real orders; handles retries, partial fills, errors.
  5. State/portfolio — knows what positions and orders really exist (always re-sync with the exchange!).
  6. Monitoring — logs, alerts (Telegram/email), dashboards, a kill switch to stop everything.

  • Backtesting — running your rules on past data to see how they would have performed.
  • Forward testing / paper trading — running them on live data without real money.

20.4 Backtesting traps (very important for builders)

Trap What it means How to avoid
Overfitting (curve fitting) Tuning settings until the past looks perfect; fails on new data Few parameters, test on unseen data (out-of-sample), walk-forward testing
Look-ahead bias Using information that wasn't available yet (e.g. today's close to trade today's open) Only use data up to the previous closed candle
Survivorship bias Testing only coins that still exist (the dead ones are gone from the list) Include delisted assets where possible
Ignoring costs No fees, spread, slippage, funding Model them pessimistically
Unrealistic fills Assuming every limit order fills Model partial/no fills
Too few trades 15 trades "prove" nothing Hundreds of trades, different market conditions

20.5 Things that break live bots

  • Internet or server down, API outage, exchange maintenance.
  • Rate limits exceeded → your requests are blocked (Part 21).
  • Wrong precision (too many decimals) → orders rejected.
  • Bot "thinks" it has a position that doesn't exist (state out of sync).
  • Duplicate orders after a retry.
  • Sudden volatility / flash crash.
  • A bug that loops and places hundreds of orders.

→ Always build: max position size, max orders per minute, daily loss limit, and a kill switch — before any strategy code.

🧒Like I'm 10

An API is a waiter in a restaurant. Your program (the customer) doesn't walk into the kitchen (the exchange's servers). It gives the waiter a clear order from the menu ("get me BTC's price", "place this order"), and the waiter brings back the answer. The API key is your membership card that proves the order is really from you.

An API (Application Programming Interface) is a defined way for programs to talk to each other.

21.2 Public vs private endpoints

  • Public — market data anyone can read (prices, candles, order book). No key needed.
  • Private — your account (balances, orders, trading). Needs an API key and a signature.

21.3 API keys and signatures

When you create an API key on Binance or MEXC you get:

  • API key — like a username; sent in a header (X-MBX-APIKEY for Binance, X-MEXC-APIKEY for MEXC).
  • Secret key — like a password; never sent, never shared, never committed to git. It's used to create a signature: an HMAC-SHA256 hash of your request, proving it came from you and wasn't changed.
  • A timestamp is included so old requests can't be replayed.
🛑Caution

API key safety rules

  1. Enable only the permissions you need: Read and Spot trading. Never enable Withdrawals for a bot.
  2. Restrict by IP address (whitelist your server's IP).
  3. Store secrets in environment variables or a secrets manager — never in code or in a public repository.
  4. Use separate keys per bot/project, and delete keys you no longer use.
  5. Start on the testnet.

21.4 Rate limits and precision rules

  • Rate limits — exchanges limit how many requests you can send per minute (Binance counts "request weight"). Exceed it and you get errors (HTTP 429) or a temporary ban (418). Use WebSockets for live data instead of polling REST constantly.
  • Exchange info / filters — each symbol has rules: minimum quantity, step size, tick size, minimum order value ("notional"). Read them from the exchange-info endpoint and round your orders accordingly.

24.5 Final reminders
